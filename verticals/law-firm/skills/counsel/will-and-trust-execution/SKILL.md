# Will and trust execution

> Written for a law practice, where a licensed attorney makes every call that is theirs. The
> rules named are New Jersey's; another state's differ, and the attorney admitted there decides.
> Nothing here is advice to a member of the public.

The signing is the only part of the plan that is ever litigated. Every formality below exists
because a court has had to decide, after the client was gone, whether the paper was theirs.

## What each instrument requires

**Will.** In writing; signed by the testator, or by another in the testator's conscious presence
and at the testator's direction; and signed by at least two individuals, each within a reasonable
time after witnessing the signing or the testator's acknowledgment of the signature or of the will
(N.J.S.A. 3B:3-2(a)). A document that fails this is still valid as a writing intended as a will if
the signature and material portions are in the testator's handwriting (3B:3-2(b)), and a
non-compliant document can be admitted on clear and convincing evidence that the decedent intended
it as a will (3B:3-3). Neither fallback is a plan; both are what the firm argues when the plan
failed.

**Self-proving affidavit.** Make the will self-proved at signing (3B:3-4): the testator and the
witnesses swear before a notary or other officer authorized to take acknowledgments, in the
statutory form, and the Surrogate can admit the will without finding the witnesses. A will not
self-proved at signing can be made so later (3B:3-5), which requires the witnesses again. There is
no reason to skip this at the ceremony and every reason not to.

**Revocable trust.** No witnessing statute governs; the trust is a contract between settlor and
trustee under the Uniform Trust Code (N.J.S.A. 3B:31-1 et seq.). Have the settlor and the initial
trustee sign and acknowledge before a notary anyway: the deed into the trust will be recorded,
institutions will ask, and an acknowledged instrument is harder to attack.

**Durable power of attorney.** In writing, signed and acknowledged in the manner of R.S. 46:14-2.1
(N.J.S.A. 46:2B-8.9). Durability requires the statutory words or their equivalent (46:2B-8.2(b)).
Revocation is by destroying all executed originals, by a signed and acknowledged written revocation,
or by delivering a written revocation to the agent; a later power does not revoke an earlier one
unless it says so (46:2B-8.10) — so revoke the old one expressly.

**Advance directive.** Signed and dated by or at the direction of the declarant, either in the
presence of two adult witnesses who attest to sound mind and freedom from duress, or acknowledged
before a notary, attorney or other oath officer. The designated health care representative may not
be a witness (N.J.S.A. 26:2H-56).

**Deed.** Signed by the grantor and acknowledged before an officer authorized to take
acknowledgments, with the consideration stated and the affidavit of consideration attached where
required; see `counsel:trust-funding` for the deed into a trust.

## Run the ceremony the same way every time

- **The attorney conducts it.** Not a paralegal alone, not a notary at a bank. The attorney's
  presence is the capacity record.
- **The client alone first.** Ask what the document is, what it does, who is named and why, in
  the client's words. Note the answers. This five minutes is the evidence of capacity and of the
  absence of pressure, and it is not optional for an older or ill client.
- **Beneficiaries out of the room** for the signing, even where the statute would tolerate them.
  A will signed with the principal beneficiary at the table is a will contest's opening exhibit.
- **Two disinterested adult witnesses**, present together, who watch the testator sign and sign
  themselves in the testator's presence. Office staff are the usual answer; neither may be a
  beneficiary or a fiduciary named in the documents, and the health care representative may not
  witness the advance directive.
- **One notary**, not a witness, for the self-proving affidavit, the power of attorney, the trust
  and any deed.
- **The attorney reads the execution pages aloud** as they are signed: name, date, place. Blanks
  are filled before signing, not after.
- **Initial every page** if the firm's practice is to; a consistent practice is what matters.
- **Conform the file copy** and record the ceremony: date, place, who was present, the capacity
  questions and answers, which originals went where.

## Remote execution

New Jersey's permanent remote online notarization law (P.L. 2021, c. 179) lets a notarial officer
act for a remotely located individual, but remote notarial acts do not apply to a tangible record
governed by the law of wills and codicils (N.J.S.A. 52:7-10.10). A will is signed in person, with
the witnesses physically present. A power of attorney, a trust or a deed may be remotely
acknowledged where the platform and the officer meet the statute's requirements; confirm the
current requirements with the Division of Revenue before offering it, and prefer in-person signing
for any document likely to be contested.

## Hospital, nursing home and home signings

Plan them as ceremonies with worse conditions. Bring the witnesses and the notary rather than
recruiting staff on site; confirm the client is alert and unmedicated enough to answer the capacity
questions before unpacking the documents; clear the room of family; and, if capacity cannot be
confirmed in the room, do not sign and say why in the file. A signing that proceeds because the
family drove an hour is the one that gets challenged.

## Originals

The original will is what the Surrogate wants, and a lost original raises a presumption of
revocation. Decide where it lives — the firm's vault, the client's safe deposit box, the client's
home — record the decision, and tell the executor. A will the executor cannot find is a will that
did not exist. For a power of attorney and an advance directive, the client holds originals and
the agent and representative hold copies, so they are usable when needed.

## Fixing a defect

A will with one witness, a missing self-proving affidavit, a signature in the wrong place: the
fixes are, in order, re-execute if the client is living and able; make self-proved later under
3B:3-5 if only the affidavit is missing; and, at death, the writing-intended-as-a-will proof under
3B:3-3 on clear and convincing evidence. Re-execution is the only one of the three that costs
nothing to prove.

## Never

- Sign a will without two disinterested witnesses present together and the self-proving affidavit before a notary.
- Let a beneficiary, a named fiduciary or the health care representative serve as a witness.
- Proceed with a signing when the client cannot say, alone, what the document does and who is in it.
- Offer remote execution for a will.
- Leave blanks to be filled after signing.
- Lose track of where the original will is.
