# Client intake and engagement

> Written for a law practice, where a licensed attorney makes every call that is theirs. The
> rules named are New Jersey's; another state's differ, and the attorney admitted there decides.
> Nothing here is advice to a member of the public.

The engagement is where most later trouble is created. A matter that starts with a clear client,
a clear scope and a written fee basis almost never produces a fee dispute or an ethics grievance.
One that starts with "we'll sort out the details" produces both.

## Check conflicts before you hear the story

Run the conflict check on every name — the prospective client, the other parties, the fiduciaries,
the beneficiaries, the lender, the other side of a closing — before the substance is discussed.
A prospective client who has told you the facts is owed confidentiality whether or not you take the
matter, and a conflict discovered after the story is told can disqualify the firm from both sides.

Keep the index of names the check runs against current. A check against a stale list is a ritual.

A hit stops the intake. The attorney decides whether it is a true conflict, a waivable one with
informed written consent from each affected client, or a false positive, and records which.

## Decide who the client is

This is the question a family intake most often skips, and it decides everything after it.

- **The caller is not automatically the client.** An adult child arranging a parent's estate plan,
  a spouse setting up a meeting, a friend who found the lawyer — none of them is the client unless
  the firm decides so. The person whose will it is, whose property it is, whose capacity is at
  issue, is the client, and the firm's duties run to them.
- **Spouses together are two clients.** Joint representation is common in estate planning and
  usually right, but it is a joint representation: each spouse's interests are the firm's concern,
  what one tells the firm is not secret from the other, and if their interests diverge the firm may
  have to withdraw from both. Say all of that in the engagement letter and get both signatures.
- **An executor is the client, not the estate's beneficiaries.** The beneficiaries will assume
  otherwise. The engagement letter says who is represented, and a short letter to beneficiaries
  who ask says who is not.
- **A business and its owner are different clients.** An LLC's operating agreement and its member's
  will are related documents with potentially adverse parties.

Write the answer into the engagement letter: who is the client, and who, by name or by role, is
not. See `legal-risk:privacy-and-data-protection` for the firm's confidentiality duty as it is
extended for a practice.

## Screen capacity and pressure at the first meeting

Estate planning and elder law clients are, by definition, often older, sometimes ill, and
frequently accompanied. New Jersey RPC 1.14 keeps a client with diminished capacity a client, to be
treated as normally as possible, and lets the attorney take protective action when the client cannot
act in their own interest.

- **See the client alone** for at least part of every substantive meeting. A relative who refuses
  to leave the room is information.
- **Ask open questions** about family, assets and what the client wants to happen, and record the
  answers in the client's own words. The record of capacity is the attorney's contemporaneous note,
  not a later recollection.
- **Watch for the pattern** that courts treat as undue influence: a beneficiary who arranged the
  meeting, drove the client, supplied the instructions, and benefits from a change to a prior plan.
  None of those is disqualifying alone. All of them together is a plan the attorney should not
  draft without more.
- **Decline when the answer is unclear.** Drafting for a client whose capacity the attorney doubts
  is worse for the client than a referral for a capacity evaluation.

## Put the fee basis in writing — it is a rule, not a courtesy

New Jersey RPC 1.5(b) requires the basis or rate of the fee to be communicated in writing, before
or within a reasonable time after the representation starts, and any later change in writing too;
the only exception is a previous client taken on again on the same fee structure. A fee that was
only discussed is a fee the firm may not be able to collect.

The engagement letter carries:

- **The client**, and who is not the client.
- **The scope**: what the firm is doing, and, as importantly, what it is not. A will-and-trust
  engagement does not include funding the trust unless it says so; an estate administration does
  not include the inheritance tax return unless it says so; a closing does not include the
  lender's work. Limited scope is permitted with the client's informed consent (RPC 1.2(c)) and is
  the honest shape of most flat-fee work.
- **The fee basis**: flat, hourly, or a stated hybrid; what is included; what is billed separately
  (filing fees, recording fees, title charges, the surrogate's fees); when payment is due.
- **Where money is held.** An advance fee the client expects to be held until earned goes in the
  trust account; if the agreement is that it is earned on receipt and may go to the business
  account, say so plainly, and understand the client can still dispute the fee.
- **How the engagement ends**: on delivery of the documents, on closing, on the final accounting,
  or on written notice; and that the firm has no continuing duty to update the plan for changes in
  law or in the client's life after it ends.
- **The file**: what the client gets, what the firm keeps, and for how long.

Flat fees need a scope they can carry. `revenue:pricing-and-packaging` is extended with how a
practice packages them.

## Decline in writing, and quickly

A prospective client the firm will not take should hear it fast and in writing — that the firm is
not representing them, that no attorney-client relationship was formed, that deadlines may be
running and they should consult another lawyer promptly. Silence after a consultation is how a
non-client comes to believe they were represented, and a missed deadline follows.

Do not state the reason if the reason is a judgment about the client. State the fact of
declination.

## Open the matter properly

Once engaged: the signed letter in the file, the client's identity verified, the conflict check
recorded with its date and result, the responsible attorney named, the first deadline computed if
any clock is already running, and the client told what happens next and when they will hear from
the firm. `customer-experience:customer-onboarding-and-implementation` is extended with what a
client needs to hear in the first week.

## Never

- Hear the facts of a matter before the conflict check has run on every name.
- Treat the person who called as the client without deciding that it is so.
- Represent two people jointly without telling both, in writing, what joint representation means.
- Start work for a new client without the fee basis in writing.
- Draft for a client whose capacity the attorney doubts.
- Leave a declined prospect without a written declination that says deadlines may be running.
