# Elder law and Medicaid planning

> Written for a law practice, where a licensed attorney makes every call that is theirs. The
> rules named are New Jersey's and federal; another state's differ, and the attorney admitted
> there decides. Nothing here is advice to a member of the public.

Medicaid planning is estate planning with a clock running backward: anything given away in the
sixty months before an application is counted as if it were still owned. Every other rule in the
field is a consequence of that one.

## The look-back and the penalty

Federal law requires the State to look back sixty months from the Medicaid application for any
transfer of assets for less than fair market value (42 U.S.C. § 1396p(c)(1)(B)). The uncompensated
value of what was transferred, divided by the State's monthly penalty divisor — a figure New Jersey
sets and updates, so take it from the current Medicaid communication rather than memory — is the
number of months of ineligibility for nursing facility care, and the penalty runs from the date the
applicant is otherwise eligible, not from the date of the gift. A gift made in month one of the
look-back and discovered at application creates a penalty that starts when the client is already
in the home and out of money.

The planning consequences:

- **A gift today is a gift that must be survived for five years.** The client who can afford to
  wait five years and the client who cannot need different plans, and the first question is which
  one is in the room.
- **Everything is a transfer** unless an exception applies: adding a child to a deed, paying a
  grandchild's tuition, forgiving a loan, selling the house to a child below value, "giving back"
  an inheritance. The annual federal gift-tax exclusion is irrelevant to Medicaid; a $19,000 gift
  is a $19,000 transfer.
- **Paying a family member for care** is not a transfer if it is pay for services actually
  rendered under a written care agreement made before the services, at a fair rate, with records.
  Without the agreement it is a gift.

## What is protected without a transfer

- **The home**, while the applicant or a spouse or a dependent relative lives there, within the
  State's equity limit. The home may be transferred without penalty to a spouse, to a child under
  21 or who is blind or disabled, to a sibling with an equity interest who lived there for a year
  before institutionalization, or to a caregiver child who lived there for two years before and
  provided care that kept the parent out of a facility (§ 1396p(c)(2)). The caregiver-child
  exception is the most valuable and the most often lost for want of evidence; build the record
  while the care is happening.
- **The community spouse's resources and income.** Federal law lets the spouse at home keep a
  protected share of the couple's countable resources and a monthly income allowance, both within
  annually adjusted limits that the State publishes. Spousal refusal and the allowance figures are
  State-specific; take the numbers from the current publication.
- **One vehicle, personal effects, a prepaid irrevocable funeral, and small life insurance** are
  excluded within the program's rules.

## Trusts, and what they cost

An **irrevocable trust** from which the settlor can receive income but not principal removes the
principal from countable resources once the look-back has run; the settlor gives up the principal
forever, the trustee controls it, and a trust the settlor can revoke or reach protects nothing.
Retained income is countable income. Drafting choices — a limited power of appointment to preserve
a step-up in basis, a provision for the trustee to pay for the settlor's benefit indirectly, who
the remainder beneficiaries are — decide whether the trust is a protection or a trap, and this is a
specialized drafting job rather than a template.

A **revocable trust** protects nothing; it is a planning tool for incapacity and probate, not for
Medicaid. A **supplemental needs trust** for a disabled beneficiary preserves that person's
eligibility and is often the right vehicle for the share of an inheritance that would otherwise
end benefits; a first-party version carries a payback to the State.

## Estate recovery

The State recovers Medicaid paid for a recipient fifty-five or older from the recipient's estate
after death, deferred while a spouse or a dependent child survives. New Jersey's recovery reaches
the probate estate and can reach more, so the plan should assume that what the recipient owned at
death is exposed. The point of the planning above is that there is less of it.

## Capacity, pressure and who the client is

Elder law intake is where `counsel:client-intake-and-engagement` matters most. The family that
calls wants assets protected; the client is the parent, and the parent's interest — to have care
paid for, to stay home as long as possible, to not be impoverished for the children's benefit — can
diverge from the family's. The attorney represents the parent, sees the parent alone, and declines
to implement a transfer the parent does not understand or want. A power of attorney without
explicit gifting authority does not let an agent make Medicaid gifts, and an agent gifting to
themselves under one that does is a conflict the document must have anticipated.

## When the nursing home has already asked

Crisis planning — the client is in the facility and private pay is running out — has fewer
options, and the attorney should say so. What remains: spend-down on exempt assets and legitimate
expenses, a care agreement for a family caregiver going forward, the spousal protections, and the
half-a-loaf techniques that pair a gift with a Medicaid-compliant annuity to fund the penalty
period. All of them need the State's current figures and a worked calculation before anyone signs.

## Never

- Recommend a gift by an older client without the five-year analysis in writing.
- State the penalty divisor, the resource allowance or the income allowance from memory.
- Treat a revocable trust as Medicaid protection.
- Let a family member be paid for care without a written agreement made before the care.
- Implement a transfer the client, seen alone, cannot explain or does not want.
- Let an agent gift under a power of attorney that does not expressly authorize it.
