# Trust funding

> Written for a law practice, where a licensed attorney makes every call that is theirs. The
> rules named are New Jersey's; another state's differ, and the attorney admitted there decides.
> Nothing here is advice to a member of the public.

A revocable trust governs only what has been transferred to it. The signing creates the trust; the
funding makes it do anything. A trust plan that ends at the signing has produced a pour-over will
with extra pages, and the family discovers it at the Surrogate's counter.

## The funding schedule is the deliverable

Produce, at signing, a schedule with one row per asset from the plan's asset map: the asset, how it
is held today, how it should be held, who does the transfer (firm or client), what document or
form does it, and the date it was confirmed done. The engagement letter says whether funding is in
scope; if it is the client's job, the schedule and a set of instructions are the deliverable and
the file records that the client was told.

Review the schedule at every later touch — an annual letter, a new purchase, a refinance — because
assets acquired after the plan arrive in the client's own name.

## Asset by asset

**Real estate.** A deed from the owner to the trustee of the trust, prepared by the firm, with the
legal description copied from the vesting deed, acknowledged, and recorded in the county. Three
things to get right:

- *The realty transfer fee.* A deed for consideration under $100 is exempt (N.J.S.A. 46:15-10(a)),
  which is how a transfer into the grantor's own revocable trust is ordinarily recorded, with the
  affidavit of consideration claiming the exemption. The Division of Taxation treats a transfer to
  a trustee holding for *other* beneficiaries differently, so claim the exemption the facts
  support and read the Division's guidance when the trust is not the grantor's own.
- *The mortgage.* Federal law bars a lender from enforcing a due-on-sale clause on a transfer into
  an inter vivos trust in which the borrower is and remains a beneficiary and which does not
  transfer occupancy rights (12 U.S.C. § 1701j-3(d)(8)). Tell the client; do not ask the lender's
  permission, but do notify the lender and the insurer so the servicing and the policy follow.
- *Title insurance and homeowner's insurance.* Ask the title insurer to endorse the policy to the
  trustee, and have the homeowner's policy name the trust as an additional insured. A loss after
  an unendorsed transfer is an argument the client does not need.

Property-tax relief programs tied to ownership and residence — senior and veteran deductions,
the senior freeze — have their own rules about trust-held property. Check the current program
rules before the deed is recorded, and tell the client what changes.

**Bank and brokerage accounts.** Retitle to the trustee, or, where the institution's forms are
simpler and the plan allows it, add a payable-on-death or transfer-on-death designation to the
trust. The institution's own form governs; collect it, complete it, confirm the account statement
shows the new title.

**Retirement accounts** — IRAs, 401(k)s, 403(b)s — are **never retitled** to the trust. Retitling
is a distribution and a taxable event. The plan is implemented by beneficiary designation, and a
designation naming the trust is a specialized choice with income-tax consequences under the
ten-year rule; the default for a spouse is usually the spouse. See the practice's extension of the
`finance` department's tax skill.

**Life insurance and annuities.** Beneficiary designation, not retitling, unless the plan moves
ownership into an irrevocable trust for estate-tax reasons, which is a different plan.

**LLC and partnership interests.** Read the operating agreement first. Most agreements restrict
transfer and distinguish an assignee, who receives distributions, from a member, who votes. An
assignment of the interest to the trustee, with the other members' consent where the agreement
requires it and an amendment admitting the trustee as a member, is the usual instrument. An
assignment the agreement forbids transfers nothing.

**Vehicles, tangible personal property.** Usually not retitled; a general assignment of tangible
personal property to the trust, signed at the ceremony, covers household contents. Vehicles are
left in the owner's name and pass under the will or by the Motor Vehicle Commission's survivorship
process.

**Business receivables, intellectual property, digital assets.** By assignment, with the registry
or platform's process where one exists.

## Confirm, then write the funding letter

Each row of the schedule is closed by evidence: the recorded deed with its book and page, the
account statement in the trustee's name, the designation confirmation from the institution, the
signed assignment. The firm sends the client a funding letter listing what was done, what remains
and who owns it, and files the evidence with the trust. The letter is the proof the family will
need, and it is what distinguishes a trust that was funded from one the client was told to fund.

## The unfunded trust at death

When a trust turns out to hold nothing, the pour-over will carries the probate estate into it after
probate, which is the outcome the trust was meant to avoid but not a failure of the plan's
dispositive scheme. Assets with no designation and no joint owner go through the Surrogate under
`counsel:estate-administration`; the trust then governs distribution. Tell the family that the
plan still works and what it will now cost in time.

## Never

- Close a trust engagement without a funding schedule that names who does each transfer.
- Retitle a retirement account to a trust.
- Record a deed into a trust without claiming the exemption the facts support on the affidavit of consideration.
- Assign an LLC interest without reading the operating agreement's transfer restrictions.
- Treat a transfer as done before the evidence is in the file.
- Tell a client the trust is funded when the firm did not confirm it.
