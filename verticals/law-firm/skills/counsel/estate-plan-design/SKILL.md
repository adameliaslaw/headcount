# Estate plan design

> Written for a law practice, where a licensed attorney makes every call that is theirs. The
> rules named are New Jersey's; another state's differ, and the attorney admitted there decides.
> Nothing here is advice to a member of the public.

A plan is a set of answers to one question asked asset by asset: who gets this, how, and what does
it cost to get there. Most bad plans are not wrong on the law; they are a will drafted before
anyone listed the assets, so the will governs property that passes outside it.

## Start with the asset map, not the documents

Before choosing instruments, list every asset with three facts: how it is titled, whether it
carries a beneficiary designation, and roughly what it is worth. The map decides the plan:

- **Passes by title**: joint accounts and real estate held jointly with right of survivorship or
  by the entirety. The will does not reach these while the joint owner survives.
- **Passes by designation**: retirement accounts, life insurance, annuities, transfer-on-death and
  payable-on-death accounts. The designation controls, whatever the will says, and a designation
  naming an ex-spouse or a predeceased parent is the most common way a plan fails.
- **Passes by trust**: whatever has been retitled into a trust, and only that.
- **Passes by will**: everything else, through probate.

The design question for each asset is which of those four it should use, and the documents follow.

## Will or revocable trust

New Jersey probate is a Surrogate's Court process rather than a supervised one, and the Surrogate
admits a will after the tenth day following death (N.J.S.A. 3B:3-22). A will-based plan is often
adequate here, and the trust is chosen for a reason rather than by default. The reasons that hold:

- **Incapacity management** of real estate and accounts without a guardianship, through the
  successor trustee. A durable power of attorney covers much of this, but institutions resist
  powers of attorney in ways they do not resist a trustee.
- **Real estate in another state**, which otherwise means a second probate there.
- **Privacy**: a probated will is a public record; a trust is not.
- **Continuity for a beneficiary who should not receive outright** — a minor, a person with a
  disability whose benefits a direct inheritance would end, a spendthrift, a second marriage with
  children from the first.
- **A blended family** where the surviving spouse's later will would otherwise control the first
  spouse's property.

A trust that is chosen is a trust that must be funded, and the engagement says whose job that is.
`counsel:trust-funding` holds the method; an unfunded trust is the most expensive piece of paper in
estate planning. Every trust plan carries a pour-over will for what was missed, and a will-based
plan carries a will.

## The four documents everyone needs

- **Will**, with executor and successor, guardian of minors and successor, the dispositive scheme,
  the residuary clause and the tax apportionment clause. Executed under N.J.S.A. 3B:3-2 and made
  self-proving under 3B:3-4 at signing, so the witnesses need not be found later.
- **Durable power of attorney**, with the durability language 46:2B-8.2(b) requires, acknowledged
  before a notary (46:2B-8.9). Decide gifting authority explicitly; a silent instrument gives none,
  and one that grants it without limit is an invitation.
- **Advance directive for health care** — a proxy directive naming the representative and an
  instruction directive stating wishes — executed under N.J.S.A. 26:2H-56 with two adult witnesses
  (not the representative) or before a notary, attorney or other oath officer. Note that
  designation of a spouse as representative is revoked by divorce or legal separation unless the
  directive says otherwise (26:2H-57(c)).
- **Beneficiary designation review**, in writing, asset by asset, with the forms the institutions
  require. This is a deliverable, not a reminder.

## Tax: New Jersey has no estate tax and does have an inheritance tax

New Jersey's estate tax ended for deaths on or after January 1, 2018. The transfer inheritance tax
remains, and it is a tax on who receives, not on how much the estate is worth:

- **Class A** — spouse, civil union partner, domestic partner, parents, grandparents, children
  (including adopted and stepchildren), grandchildren and further descendants — pay nothing.
- **Class C** — siblings, and a child's spouse or surviving spouse — pay nothing on the first
  $25,000, then 11% up to $1,100,000, 13% to $1,400,000, 14% to $1,700,000 and 16% above
  (N.J.S.A. 54:34-2(c)).
- **Class D** — everyone else, including nieces, nephews, friends and unmarried partners who are
  not registered domestic partners — pay 15% up to $700,000 and 16% above (54:34-2(d)).
- **Class E** — qualified charities, religious and educational institutions, the State — pay
  nothing (54:34-4(d)).

Life insurance paid to a named beneficiary other than the estate is exempt whatever the class
(54:34-4(f)). The return and the tax are due eight months after death, with no weekend or holiday
carry-over, and the tax is a lien on the decedent's property for fifteen years (54:35-5). Tax
waivers are needed to transfer New Jersey real estate and certain accounts; `counsel:estate-administration`
holds the mechanics.

The design consequence: a gift to a sibling, a niece or a partner is a taxable gift at death that
a gift to a child is not, and the client should be told the number. Where the client wants to
benefit a Class D person, lifetime gifts, life insurance, and the choice of which asset carries
the gift all change what the State takes.

**Federal estate and gift tax.** For 2026 the basic exclusion amount is $15,000,000 per person
(Rev. Proc. 2025-32, under the 2025 federal tax law), indexed thereafter, and the annual gift
exclusion is $19,000 per donee. Confirm the current year's figures from the IRS before they go in a
letter. For a married couple, portability of the deceased spouse's unused exclusion requires a
timely Form 706 even when no tax is due — a decision to be made in administration, and one the
plan should anticipate. Few New Jersey clients face federal tax; every client faces the state's
inheritance tax if they leave anything outside Class A.

## Constraints the plan must respect

- **The elective share.** A surviving spouse, civil union partner or domestic partner may elect
  one-third of the augmented estate (N.J.S.A. 3B:8-1), subject to the statute's conditions and
  exceptions. A plan that disinherits a spouse without a waiver — typically a prenuptial or
  marital agreement — is a plan for litigation.
- **Retirement accounts.** A non-spouse beneficiary of an inherited IRA or plan generally must
  empty it within ten years of the owner's death (26 U.S.C. § 401(a)(9)(H)), with exceptions for
  eligible designated beneficiaries including a spouse, a minor child until majority, and a
  disabled or chronically ill individual. Naming a trust as beneficiary is possible and is a
  specialized drafting job; naming the estate is usually the worst outcome. See the `finance`
  department's tax skill as extended for the practice.
- **Survivorship.** For intestate succession an heir must survive the decedent by 120 hours
  (3B:5-1); a will should state its own survivorship condition rather than rely on the default.
- **Interested witnesses.** A will is not invalid because a beneficiary witnessed it (3B:3-8), but
  the plan should never need the point; use disinterested witnesses.

## Special situations worth a separate conversation

- **A beneficiary on means-tested benefits** needs a supplemental needs trust, not an outright
  gift that ends the benefits.
- **A client who may need long-term care** within five years needs the Medicaid analysis before
  gifting anything; `counsel:elder-law-and-medicaid-planning`.
- **A business or LLC interest** passes under the operating agreement's transfer rules before it
  passes under the will; read the agreement.
- **Real estate in another state** means ancillary probate or a trust.
- **Minor children** need a guardian nomination and a mechanism — a trust or a custodial account —
  so that an inheritance is not paid to a minor outright.

## Explain the plan in one page

The client gets a plain-language summary: what each document does, who is named in each role, how
each asset passes, what the State will take and from whom, and what the client must do next —
sign, fund, update designations. The summary is what the family reads first at a death; write it
for that moment.

## Never

- Draft a will before the asset map shows what the will actually governs.
- Recommend a revocable trust without a stated reason and a named owner for funding it.
- Leave a beneficiary designation unreviewed because it is not a document the firm drafts.
- State a tax figure from memory; confirm the year's number against the publisher first.
- Disinherit a spouse without addressing the elective share in writing.
- Let a minor, a person on means-tested benefits, or an estate be the default taker of a retirement account.
