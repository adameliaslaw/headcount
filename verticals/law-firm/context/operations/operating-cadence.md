## The weekly docket is the practice's weekly meeting

A practice's execution review has one agenda: the deadline register. Every computed date — the
attorney review period on a contract, the notice of probate, the creditor claim period, the
inheritance tax return, a Form 706, a contingency in a contract, a court date — sits in one
register with its trigger fact, the rule and version it was computed under, the owner, and the
status. The weekly meeting walks every date inside the next sixty days and asks three questions:
is the trigger fact still right, has the work that the date needs started, and who is doing it.

- **One set of dates, computed, owned by name.** A date typed into a calendar by hand is not in
  the register, and a date in the register that was not computed from a rule is a finding.
- **Monthly** adds the trust account reconciliation (signed, under R. 1:21-6), the aged-matter
  review (every matter with no activity in thirty days gets a reason or a next step), and work
  in progress against the flat-fee scope — the practice's version of run-rate versus timing.
- **Quarterly** reviews the rules themselves. New Jersey's court rules are amended by the Supreme
  Court's annual omnibus order, ordinarily effective September 1; tax figures change each January;
  the Division of Taxation revises forms on its own schedule. A rule version the firm computes
  from is checked against the publisher and re-pinned, and every open date that depends on it is
  recomputed.
- **What jumps the cadence:** a date inside ten days with no owner or no started work escalates to
  the managing attorney the day it is noticed, not at the next weekly meeting. A capacity concern
  at a signing, a conflict hit, a bounced trust check and a client complaint do the same.

The pruning rule applies in reverse here. The docket meeting is the one forum a practice may not
cancel, and a week it is skipped is a week a date can pass unseen.
