## Onboarding a client to a practice

Signature to live, in a practice, is engagement letter to a plan that is signed and funded, an
estate that is closed, or a deed that is recorded. The core's method holds — define live before
the engagement, name the owner, sequence for early value — with the client as the customer-side
owner and the rules of professional conduct setting the communication floor.

- **The first-week letter.** Within days of the engagement: who the responsible attorney is and
  who else will be in contact, what the firm is doing and not doing, what happens next and when
  the client will hear, what the firm needs from the client (the asset list, deeds, account
  statements and beneficiary designations for a plan; death certificates, the original will and
  the asset list for an administration; the contract and the lender's contact for a closing), and
  how to reach the firm. It restates who the client is and who is not, because the family will
  ask.
- **Define live per engagement.** A plan is live when the documents are signed, the trust is
  funded on the schedule, the designations are confirmed and the client holds the one-page
  summary. An administration is live at the fiduciary's release and the closing of the file. A
  closing is live when the deed and mortgage are recorded and the funds have cleared. Say which
  at the start.
- **Keep the client reasonably informed** (RPC 1.4) — a status note at each milestone and a
  prompt answer to a question, rather than silence between the signing and the funding letter.
  The stalled implementation the core warns about is, here, the client who stops sending the
  documents the firm asked for; chase it on a cadence and record each request.
- **Train for the job.** The client learns what the agent under the power of attorney may do,
  where the originals are, and what to do at a death; the executor learns the first two weeks.
  That is the task training; the documents are the buttons.
- **Hand over deliberately.** The engagement ends in writing: what was done, what remains and who
  owns it, where the originals are, that the firm has no continuing duty to update the plan, and
  what events — a move, a marriage, a death, a new asset — should bring the client back.
